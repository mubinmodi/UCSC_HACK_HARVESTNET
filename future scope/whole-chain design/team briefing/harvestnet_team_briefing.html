HarvestNet

Every organization keeps its own keys. The food still moves.

A network where independent organizations across the food supply chain negotiate what happens to surplus food: who receives it, how it moves, and who pays. Each organization has its own AI negotiator, backed by calculators. Nobody in the middle decides for anyone else.

01 · The problem

Good food is wasted because too many separate yeses are needed at once

Surplus food often goes to waste even though people nearby need it. The cause is usually not a lack of food. It's that several independent organizations must all agree at the same time:

  • The farm won't pay to pick a crop unless someone covers the cost.
  • The food bank has limited cold space, volunteers, and budget.
  • The truck must be the right kind and free at the right time. Some foods need cold; some, like tomatoes, are damaged by it.
  • Someone has to pay, and grant money is limited.

Today this is arranged by phone and email. When one piece changes, such as a truck breaking down, the whole plan can fall apart, and the food is lost.

Existing tools mostly list surplus and let whoever claims first take it, or use one central matcher that decides for everyone. HarvestNet takes a different approach: the organizations negotiate with each other directly, and each one keeps control of its own money, food, and schedule.

02 · The challenge

We are entering Level 3: collective intelligence

Cisco's challenge, "When AI Agents Disagree," asks teams to choose one of three levels. We claim Level 3: at least three independent agents with competing goals negotiate and try to reach agreement without a central decision-maker. The other two levels appear inside our project as evidence.

LevelWhat it asksWhere it shows up in HarvestNet
Level 1: AI teamSpecialized agents collaborate on a taskSpecialists inside each organization (cost, storage, route) produce assessments that can disagree before the negotiator decides (sections 06–09)
Level 2: Adaptive teamDiscover collaborators, adapt to failure, explain choicesWhen a truck fails, a Directory search finds replacements, with a reason card for every candidate (section 12)
Level 3: Collective intelligenceCompeting agents negotiate with no central decider; show the effect of new information; compare to a baseline; know when to stop or ask a humanOrganizations negotiate one shared draft agreement per event, adapt after failures, and results are compared with first-come-first-served (sections 10–13, 19)

Judges score functionality, innovation, agent intelligence, engineering, demonstration, and learning and evidence. We must submit a working demo, an architecture overview, implementation details, and results with lessons learned.

The coordinator: our first user

A person at a food-rescue nonprofit who watches the network. Their screen has a needs-attention list, a network map, an event panel showing each change with who made it and why, a click-through into any organization's permitted explanations, and a results strip. They contact the responsible people. They decide nothing on anyone's behalf.

Humans who approve

Each organization has its own approvers: the farmer for costs and field work, a food bank manager for large deliveries, each carrier for its reservation, the fund for its spending, and a food-safety reviewer for anything uncertain. A deal can't become final without them.

04 · The solution

Each organization gets its own AI negotiator, and nobody sits above them

Every organization runs its own negotiator, backed by specialists that check facts with calculators. The negotiators talk through a shared network and edit one shared draft agreement per surplus event. Each accepts or rejects for its own organization only. A deal becomes final, a commitment, only when every organization named in it approves the exact same version and every resource hold is still valid.

Works for any food

Nothing about a specific crop is built into the agents. Every food has a food profile (section 15). Change the food and the decisions change, because the agents read the profile.

Covers the whole chain

The demo runs four surplus events at once: farm strawberries, rejected distributor lettuce, grocery dairy, and restaurant meals. A network map shows every supply-chain stage, labeled as demonstrated, profile-ready, or proposed.

Disagreement is allowed

If organizations can't all agree, the result is a preserved disagreement: the coordinator sees the options and each side's reasons. Nothing is ever committed automatically.

Different from coffeeAGNTCY

The organizers' Lungo demo has a supervisor agent directing the others. We use the same AGNTCY building blocks but remove the supervisor and give every organization its own interests, so they have to negotiate.

What is frozen, and what changes with every run

The design separates rules that must always hold from things that vary. The rules are frozen as v1; everything else is a versioned input, and the outcome is computed by each run, not scripted.

05 · Architecture: the full network

Separate organizations, one communication room, no central decider

Diagram labels: L an LLM role, D a deterministic calculator that gives the same answer every time, W ordinary workflow, adapter, or storage, H an authorized human. In Codex's diagrams, "internal subagents" means the specialists; a specialist doesn't have to be another LLM.

How to read it

  • Food sources (farm, distributor, grocery, restaurant) and partners (recipients, carriers, cross-dock, processor, fund, advocate) each run as a separate private program with its own identity, private data, local workflow, and authority over its own resources.
  • The Cisco AGNTCY network in the middle has three parts. The Directory holds capability records and returns candidates. The room carries A2A messages over SLIM for each event. Message ordering keeps everyone seeing the same order. None of them can allocate anything.
  • The farmer reaches the farm's negotiator through ElevenLabs (speech to text, plus a spoken read-back to confirm facts).
  • Every message lands in shared records: versioned drafts, decisions with who made them, and outcomes. The coordinator screen reads from these records.
  • AGNTCY Observe / OpenTelemetry collects redacted traces and usage. The evaluation harness uses the records and traces for replay, injected faults, baselines, and coverage.
  • Beeceptor and n8n sit inside each organization (Diagram 2). They are not network-wide services that own anyone's budget or approvals.

06 · Inside every organization

The same blueprint runs separately inside every organization

This blueprint is copied once for the farm, each recipient, each carrier, the cross-dock, the processor, the fund, the distributor, the grocery store, and the restaurant. Each copy has its own identity, tools, state, and authority. LangGraph runs the local workflow; no extra LLM supervisor is needed.

Every component, in the order a message meets it

  1. Ingress checks W

    Is the sender who it claims to be? Is the message in the right format? Is it a duplicate? Does it refer to the current draft version?

  2. LangGraph local workflow W

    Runs the fixed order: assess, propose, validate, approve. It reads and updates the organization's private state: budgets, preferences, evidence, and its local log.

  3. Inputs to the specialists

    Three sources: the organization's own Beeceptor endpoints (labeled mock inventory, fleet, or finance systems); public-data adapters (USDA AMS, OSRM, Food Access Atlas, as needed); and versioned food profiles plus local policy.

  4. Internal specialists D

    Each produces facts, feasible options, and reasons for one concern (sections 07–09 list them per organization).

  5. Negotiator L

    Suggests terms and explains choices, using only the specialists' results. Any quantity or trade-off it proposes is recalculated before anything leaves.

  6. Local validator D

    Checks evidence, units, feasibility, and authority. If invalid, the suggestion goes back to be revised or turned into a refusal. A valid proposal or refusal goes to the outbox. A candidate commitment goes to the hold ledger.

  7. Owner's hold ledger W

    Places an atomic local reservation on the organization's own resource (truck time, cold space, crew, or money), tied to this draft version, with an expiry.

  8. Own n8n workflow and approver W H

    Sends the exact terms to the organization's own human, who approves or denies the current terms. The decision comes back through an authenticated callback.

  9. Decision recorder W

    Rechecks the version, evidence, and holds. If the terms have gone stale or the human denied them, it returns to the workflow. A current approval or a reasoned denial goes to the outbox.

  10. Validated outbox W

    Sends the attributed message, with a retry identity so duplicates can't create a second commitment, over A2A/SLIM.

When does a deal become final? Only when all required current approvals and valid holds are present. Transport success, an n8n callback, or one organization's approval alone is not enough. The final check records an already authorized agreement; it never chooses one. If a confirmation is uncertain, the resources stay unavailable until reconciled.

No hallucinated numbers (user decision, ADR 0002):

  • Calculators run first and hand the AI labeled results.
  • If the AI needs another number, it must call a calculator tool with a strict schema. It never does math in its own text.
  • Every number in an outgoing message must match a calculator result or sourced fact for the current version, or the message is blocked.
  • After two failures, the organization sends a template sentence built only from calculator results. The failure is logged and counted.

The advocate uses the same ingress, assessment, validation, and outbox pattern, but has no holds and no acceptance vote.

Trucks, the cross-dock, and the processor

Trucker (each carrier separately)

Negotiator: Claude Sonnet 5.5 Proposed
Job
Offer service, quote trips, grant or decline holds, and report disruptions.
Specialists
  • Fleet: availability, capacity, driver limits.
  • Telemetry: cargo temperature exposure, checked against the food profile.
  • Route: OSRM drive time plus loading and return allowances.

Says yes when
The quote covers the trip and the hold fits its schedule without overlapping another commitment.

Cross-dock

Negotiator: Claude Sonnet 5.5 Proposed
Job
Offer cold storage between two legs of a trip, for example when a distributor must clear its dock before a pantry can receive.
Specialists
  • Occupancy: space and dock use over time.
  • Compatibility: food profiles, storage zones, handling.
  • Connection: incoming and onward commitments.
  • Cost: handling and storage charges.

There's compatible space for the whole interval and the onward leg and receiving are already secured.

Processor

Negotiator: Claude Sonnet 5.5 Proposed
Job
Bid to buy input it can use, and arrange collection.
Specialists
  • Input: the actual lot against its input specification.
  • Process: capacity, readiness, sourced yield.
  • Budget: purchase and processing economics.
Says yes when
The lot meets its specification at a price below its value. Raw input weight is never counted as finished product or donated food.

How discovery works. Discovery is a procedure inside the organization that needs a service, not a central LLM:

  1. Query the AGNTCY Directory with capability requirements.
  2. Ask each candidate for its own current quote.
  3. Assess the candidates.
  4. Write a reason card for every one, chosen or rejected.

The requester proposes a choice; the chosen provider still controls its own hold and approval.

Packers, animal-feed recipients, and compost operators are proposed expansion roles. They reuse the same blueprint with their own eligibility, capacity, timing, cost, and handling assessments, but those aren't claimed as ready.

10 · Agent orchestration

Who coordinates the agents? No one above them

Inside an organization

A LangGraph workflow runs Diagram 2 in a fixed order. It's the organization's own process and acts only for that organization.

Between organizations

No conductor. Coordination comes from shared rules everyone follows: one draft agreement per event, a published turn order for amendments, holds controlled by each resource's owner, and approval of the exact same version by every named organization.

Holds, versions, and running several events at once

  • A hold is placed only by a resource's owner and is tied to one event, one draft version, a quantity, an occupied time interval, and an expiry.
  • Each owner checks all its events for overlaps, so two deals can't book the same truck or spend the same dollar.
  • A declared acquisition and release procedure keeps a deal from holding half its resources forever.
  • Any change to the terms creates a new version, and the affected organizations must approve again. Old approvals don't carry over.
  • A missing acknowledgment is not proof something failed. Duplicate or delayed messages must not create duplicate commitments. Uncertain resources are frozen until reconciled, and an existing commitment is never released just because a timer ran out.

11 · Workflow A

Voice to a checked surplus offer

  1. The farmer calls

    For example: "About four pallets of strawberries, some soft, need them gone by Thursday." ElevenLabs turns the audio into a transcript with a source reference.

  2. The intake role drafts facts

    The LLM pulls out quantities, units, dates, and location, and lists anything missing. It never guesses a missing number.

  3. Calculators check the draft

    The farm's specialists and validator return checked fields or say what needs clarifying.

  4. Spoken read-back

    ElevenLabs reads the exact checked facts back ("I heard 10,000 lb, about 1,000 lb soft, pickup Thursday. Correct?"). The farmer confirms or corrects, and corrections loop back for revalidation.

  5. Assessment and offer

    Once confirmed, the farm's market, cost, readiness, and handling specialists assess the lot, and the farm sends a validated surplus offer, with sources, over AGNTCY.

Confirming the facts only authorizes the reported facts. Approving a commitment happens later, through the farm's approval workflow.

12 · Workflow B

Disruption, discovery, and renewed approvals

  1. A reading arrives

    The carrier's Beeceptor endpoint delivers a labeled temperature reading with cargo identity and time. The telemetry specialist checks it against the food profile, the actual exposure, and which resources are affected. One warm reading in an empty truck doesn't prove the food was exposed.

  2. If the evidence is uncertain

    The affected action is held, and a human inspects it through n8n.

  3. If the service is unavailable

    The carrier sends a validated disruption with evidence. Every affected draft, hold, and commitment is notified. Completed work is preserved, and affected resources are reconciled.

  4. Discovery

    The affected organization queries the Directory with capability, region, handling, and capacity requirements, then asks each candidate for current availability and a quote. Each candidate answers with its own terms or refuses.

  5. Reason cards

    Route, handling, and timing checks run on every candidate, and every candidate gets a reason card, for example "Truck C: refrigerated, but booked until Friday."

  6. New version and re-approval

    A new draft version proposes the replacement. Every organization with obligations reassesses, reserves its resources against this version, and its human approves or denies through its own n8n workflow. The decision comes back through an authenticated callback and is revalidated.

  7. Commit, amend, or stop

    If all required approvals and holds are current, the commitment is recorded and execution notices go out, such as the crew's spoken shift notice. Otherwise organizations amend again or stop by policy, possibly recording a preserved disagreement.

Donation receipts need actual custody and receipt evidence, not just an approved plan. The n8n callbacks and service identities still need to be verified in the build.

13 · Workflow C

Shared funding and preserved disagreement

  1. Requests arrive from several events

    Each carries the current draft, the recipient's conditional acceptance, and the eligible funding gap.

  2. The fund checks policy and cash

    Eligibility, urgency, and coverage under its versioned policy. Then cash, earmarks, and existing obligations, so no dollar is promised twice.

  3. Provisional grant hold

    The fund places an expiring hold and sends full or partial grant terms with reasons.

  4. Participants recalculate and approve

    If feasible terms are accepted, every organization approves its own obligations. If all approvals and holds are valid, the commitment is recorded and the grant is reserved, not yet spent.

  5. Refusal or stop

    A declined partial grant is released, and the fund reconsiders other known requests. If the stopping policy is reached, the coordinator sees the options and the disagreement.

Candidate fund rule (Round 10, with Codex's Round 11 corrections) Proposed

  • Mandate: urgent eligible transfers first, then least-served communities Decided.
  • Eligibility uses the recipient's conditional acceptance of current terms. Requiring a final commitment first would be circular.
  • Timing: decide at each request's funding-needed-by time, derived from remaining approval and preparation steps, without counting lead time twice.
  • Ranking: urgency band, then the least-covered recipient, then lower grant per share of request covered, then a fixed tie-break.
  • Earmarks for higher-ranked known requests are explicit expiring holds, never extra money.
  • Partial awards need a feasible amended agreement. A refusal releases the hold and triggers reconsideration of other requests.
  • Limitations: requests that arrive later are unknown, so arrival order can still matter. The coverage cohort must count zeros and resist request-splitting.

15 · Food profiles

How one architecture handles any food

A food profile describes a product at the needed level of detail (crop, maturity, processing state, or recipe):

  • temperature and exposure requirements, and chilling or freezing sensitivity
  • shelf-life assumptions and preparation needs
  • packaging and unit conversions, and which foods it's compatible with
  • quality categories, candidate pathways, and the specialist assessments it requires

Every field records its source or marks itself as an assumption, and has a version. Specialists apply common procedures to these properties, and negotiator rules never branch on crop names.

Event and profile familyWhat must be assessedSources and status
Farm strawberriesIn field or harvested; maturity and condition; picking, packing, prompt cooling; packaging; exposure history; transport compatibility; receiving or processor specsUC Davis strawberry guidance and USDA Agriculture Handbook 66. Lot condition, crew throughput, cooling capacity, and quotes need local records or labeled test values
Distributor lettuceVariety; whole vs. processed; packaging; cooling and exposure; freezing and ethylene sensitivity; storage; receiving windows; documented rejection reasonUC Davis crisphead and romaine guidance, Handbook 66. A whole-head profile can't authorize fresh-cut product
Grocery dairyExact product and package; label and lot identity; holding requirements; exposure and date records; package integrity; recipient acceptanceProduct and operator specs, FDA donation guidance. No single dairy shelf life applies to every product
Restaurant mealsRecipe, ingredients, allergens; preparation and holding records; hot or cold path; packaging; portions; serving deadlineOperator records and FDA donation guidance. Needs a recipe-specific profile
  • What isn't a food property: a farm's preparation ceiling, budget, warehouse space, and prices belong to the organization or to observations.
  • Profiles aren't eligibility: a soft sublot doesn't automatically qualify for processing, and a refrigerated truck doesn't automatically fit every crop.
  • Generality tests include a chilling-sensitive tomato lot. Changing only the crop name isn't a real test.
  • Unsupported foods must produce an explicit "unsupported" result.

What we measure

  • Headline: worst-served coverage, the lowest received share in the declared recipient cohort, including zeros, defined before the run.
  • Offered, proposed, committed, received, projected, and actually distributed quantities; pounds by final pathway; unharvested or unclaimed food.
  • Quoted, reserved, authorized, and incurred costs; released funds; uncovered expenses.
  • Valid proposal and agreement rates, refusals, missed deadlines, rejected invalid proposals, human interventions, disclosure, number-check failures.
  • Negotiation time and rounds, API and model failures, retries, and run-to-run variation.

Passing requires zero invalid or unauthorized commitments, no duplicated resources or food, traceable numbers, reconciled uncertain actions, and every run ending with a reason. A system that rejects everything may be safe but still fails on usefulness. We report whatever the numbers show, including losses against the baseline.

22 · Glossary

Words we use, and what they mean

Organization
An independent participant that controls its own resources and approves its own commitments.
Negotiator
An organization's AI representative that talks to other organizations and explains its decisions.
Specialist
A calculator inside one organization that checks one thing, such as cost, storage, or route.
Coordinator
The person who watches the network. Decides nothing for anyone. Our first user.
Surplus lot
A specific batch of at-risk food, such as 10,000 lb of strawberries in one field.
Event
One surplus lot's journey from first offer to final outcome.
Food profile
The sourced, versioned facts about one kind of food: handling, chilling sensitivity, usable life, packaging, compatibility, grades, pathways, and required assessments.
Draft agreement
The one shared, numbered plan for an event, edited until everyone accepts the same version.
Hold
A temporary, expiring reservation of truck time, cold space, crew, or money, placed by the resource's owner.
Commitment
Final: every named organization approved the exact same version, with valid holds.
Preserved disagreement
No version won everyone's approval. The coordinator sees the options and reasons; nobody picks.
Pathway
Where the food ends up: donated, upcycled, animal feed, compost, or left unharvested. Each pound counted once.
Coverage
The share of a recipient's declared request it received. Worst-served coverage is our headline measure.
Walk-away option
What an organization gets with no deal. A deal must beat it.
Harness
The controlled environment around the negotiators: tools, inputs, drafts, holds, records, and replay.
Reason card
A short explanation of why a discovered candidate was chosen or rejected.
